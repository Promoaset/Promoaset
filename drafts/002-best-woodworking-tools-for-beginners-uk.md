You do not need a full workshop to start woodworking. For most first projects, a reliable measuring setup, a way to hold the work securely, one suitable cutting tool and a safe place to work will do more for you than a trolley full of specialist equipment.

This guide is a buying framework rather than a list of one “best” brand. ## The starter kit I would build first

### 1. Measuring and marking tools

Begin with a tape measure, a combination square and a sharp pencil. Add a marking knife later if you begin making joints where a fine, repeatable line matters.

A square is useful for checking whether an edge or cut is at a right angle. Before buying, look for a readable scale, a head that sits firmly against the work and a size that suits the projects you expect to make. A small square is convenient for boxes and shelves, while a longer straightedge can help with larger sheet goods.

The important buying decision is not the number of markings on the tool. It is whether you can read it easily, hold it consistently and keep it clean enough to remain accurate.

### 2. A hand saw suited to the work

A general-purpose handsaw can cover simple cuts in softwood and board. A finer-toothed backsaw may suit smaller pieces and more controlled work, but it is less useful if your first projects involve long cuts in sheet material.

Choose the saw around the material and cut you expect to make. Check that the handle feels secure, the blade is straight and replacement blades or a replacement saw are available from a UK retailer. Do not assume that a more aggressive saw is automatically better; a tool that removes material quickly can also be harder to control for a beginner.

If you are considering a powered saw, treat it as a separate safety decision. Read the manufacturer’s instructions, consider how the work will be supported, and plan dust control before buying the machine.

### 3. A drill and driver, only when your projects need one

A cordless drill-driver is useful for pilot holes, screws and some basic hardware. For a small workshop, battery compatibility can matter more than headline power because batteries, chargers and accessories add to the total cost.

Check the tool’s chuck size, speed settings, clutch control, battery availability and the manufacturer’s intended use. A drill is not a substitute for a proper driver bit, a suitable pilot hole or a stable workholding setup. Use the right bit for the material and keep the work secured rather than holding a small piece in your hand.

For occasional shelf or box projects, you may need only a modest drill-driver and a small set of bits. Buying a larger system before you know your workload can leave money tied up in batteries and accessories you do not use.

### 4. Clamps and a stable work surface

Clamps are often more useful than another cutting tool. They hold a board while you mark or drill, keep an assembly together while glue cures and make it easier to work with both hands safely.

Start with two or four clamps that can reach across the work you actually make. Check the throat depth and maximum opening, not just the advertised clamping force. Add longer clamps after a project shows that you need them. Place scrap wood or suitable pads between the clamp and a finished surface when pressure could mark the work.

A stable bench, workmate or other secure support is part of the tool setup. The surface should not rock, and the work should not slide into the cutting path. A cheap tool used on a secure surface is usually a better starting point than an expensive tool used on an unstable table.

### 5. Sanding supplies and basic maintenance

A small selection of abrasive sheets is enough for many first projects. Keep a few grades rather than buying a large assortment before you understand your finishing process. Start with the least aggressive abrasive that will do the job, then change grade as the surface improves.

A sanding block helps keep the abrasive flatter on broad faces. A simple brush or vacuum helps remove dust from the work and bench. If you later buy a random-orbit sander, treat extraction as part of the purchase rather than an optional extra.

A blunt saw, damaged cable, loose guard or worn abrasive can change how a tool behaves. Include basic inspection and cleaning in the workflow, and follow the maintenance instructions supplied with the tool.

## Hand tools or power tools first?

Hand tools are usually quieter and can suit a shared or small space, but they still need technique, sharp edges and secure workholding. Power tools can save time, but they bring additional noise, dust, moving parts and storage requirements.

For a first project, choose the method you can use safely and repeatably. If a hand saw, drill-driver and clamps can complete the project, there may be no need to buy a powered saw yet. If cutting sheet material is becoming the repeated bottleneck, then the next purchase should be based on that specific task rather than on a generic “complete workshop” list.

## What I would choose first

For a beginner making small shelves, planters or boxes, I would start with measuring and marking tools, a general-purpose handsaw, clamps, sanding supplies, a stable work surface and suitable personal protective equipment. I would add a drill-driver when a project requires fixings, then upgrade only after a repeated task shows that a more specialised tool would save time or improve accuracy.

That order keeps the initial spend focused on tools that support several project types. It also leaves room in the budget for timber, fasteners, abrasives and safe storage, which are easy to overlook when comparing tool prices.

## Safety note: plan dust control before the tool arrives

HSE says wood dust can cause serious health problems, including asthma, and that hardwood dust can cause cancer, particularly of the nose. HSE also states that cutting and sanding wood with power tools can produce significant dust, and recommends assessing the risk and using appropriate controls.[1] [2]

For a small workshop, think about where the dust will go before you start. HSE guidance describes on-tool extraction, suitable extraction units, tight connections and maintenance as important controls. It also warns against sweeping or using compressed air in ways that disturb settled dust.[1] [2]

Read the instructions for the particular tool and extraction system. If respiratory protective equipment is needed, HSE says the correct type, fit and maintenance matter; tight-fitting masks require appropriate fit testing.[2] Do not treat a disposable mask as a universal answer to poor extraction or an unsuitable workspace.

Powered machinery also requires proper familiarisation and safe working practices. HSE highlights the importance of training, correct guards and protection devices, dust extraction and the correct tooling.[3]
